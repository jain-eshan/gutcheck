# Study types

Nine archetypes. Pick one in `design.md` step 2. Each gives the questions worth asking, where the evidence comes from, what the output looks like, and how this kind of study usually goes wrong.

Everything here sits on top of the GUTCHECK REPORT in `research.md`. The report is the core; the bigger studies wrap sections around it.

---

## 1. Quick check — the default

**When:** a single question, low stakes, a hunch to settle. Most requests.

**Questions:** just the one, as a testable claim.

**Evidence:** the default tier. Trends, related queries, Reddit, news, two or three web searches, plus whatever the question type calls for.

**Output:** the GUTCHECK REPORT. Nothing else.

**Effort:** 5-10 minutes, one phase, no plan shown.

**Goes wrong when:** you turn it into a research programme. If they asked a small question, answer it.

---

## 2. Opportunity validation — "should I build this"

**When:** someone is deciding whether to spend real time or money building something.

**Questions:**
1. Is the problem real and frequent enough that people act on it today?
2. What do they use now, and what does it cost them (money, time, irritation)?
3. Who already serves this, how well, and what do their users complain about?
4. Which narrow group has it worst, and can they be reached?
5. Is anything changing (rules, platforms, costs) that opens a window now?

**Evidence:** Reddit and review sites for complaints and workarounds; App Store or web search for incumbents; Trends for direction; builder activity for who else sees it; news for the window.

**Output:** GUTCHECK REPORT, plus **Where the opening is** (the narrow group and the wedge) and **What would kill this** (the two or three things that would make it a bad idea).

**Effort:** 15-25 minutes, two phases (demand, then competition).

**Goes wrong when:** it confirms the category is big and calls that demand. Big and settled is a reason not to build. Name the wedge or say there isn't one.

## 3. Case competition

**When:** a case prompt with a jury, a deck, and a deadline. The audience is judges who will push back on numbers.

**Questions:**
1. What is the industry's structure: size, growth, who has the power, how money flows?
2. Who are the named players, with real figures, and how do they differ?
3. What is genuinely changing right now (regulation, tech, behaviour, costs)?
4. What does the customer in this case actually want, in evidence rather than assertion?
5. What does the evidence support recommending, and what would it cost to do?

**Evidence:** regulator and industry-body pages first (they publish free, citable numbers), then company sites and annual reports, then Trends for behaviour shifts, news for the last 12 months, Reddit and reviews for the customer voice. Every number needs a source a judge can look up.

**Output:** a short brief, not a deck: **Industry at a glance** (5-8 cited numbers), **Players** (table: who, model, scale, edge), **What's changing**, **Customer evidence** (quotes and behaviour), **Recommendation** with the reasoning chain, **Risks and rebuttals** (what a judge will attack, and the answer), **Sources** (every claim, with links).

**Effort:** 30-45 minutes, three phases, plan shown.

**Goes wrong when:** market-size numbers get invented or laundered from a listicle. Cite or estimate openly with your assumptions shown. Judges kill teams on unsourced numbers, so flag anything you couldn't source.

## 4. Business plan or investment memo

**When:** the output is a document someone will commit money against.

**Questions:**
1. What problem, for whom, and what do they do today?
2. How big is this in a defensible way, built bottom-up from countable things?
3. Who competes, and why does anyone switch?
4. How would this reach customers, and what does that cost?
5. What has to be true for this to work, and which of those is least proven?

**Evidence:** everything in opportunity validation, plus pricing pages for revenue logic, job ads and funding news for competitor scale, regulator or association data for the denominator in the sizing.

**Output:** GUTCHECK REPORT plus **Market size** (bottom-up, assumptions listed line by line, sources named), **Competition** (table), **Go-to-market evidence** (where these customers already gather), **Key assumptions** ranked by how load-bearing and how proven each is, and **What to test first**.

**Effort:** 30-45 minutes, three phases, plan shown.

**Goes wrong when:** it produces a top-down market size ("1% of a $10bn market"). Build from countable units: how many of these people exist, what share plausibly buys, at what price. Show the arithmetic so someone can argue with it.

## 5. Market landscape scan

**When:** entering or mapping a category. "Who's out there, how do they make money, where's the white space?"

**Questions:**
1. Who are the players, grouped by approach rather than listed?
2. What does each charge, and what does pricing reveal about the customer?
3. Which segments are well served, and which are ignored?
4. Where is money and attention moving?

**Evidence:** web search on pricing and comparison pages; App Store for consumer; GitHub and Hacker News for tooling; news for funding and launches; review sites for what each group's users hate.

**Output:** **Map** (groups, with players and one line each), **Pricing picture**, **Underserved segments** with the evidence, **Where it's heading**, then the standard verdict and bottom line.

**Effort:** 20-30 minutes, two phases.

**Goes wrong when:** it becomes a list of companies. The value is the grouping and the gap, not the inventory.

## 6. Problem diagnosis

**When:** "is it just me", "why does this keep happening", "what actually works".

**Questions:**
1. How common is this, honestly?
2. How do people describe it in their own words, and how bad does it get?
3. What have people tried, what works, and what only appears to?
4. Is the real blocker knowledge, tools, money, or nerve?

**Evidence:** Reddit hardest and first, phrased the way a frustrated person types it; web search for advice threads and fixes; Trends only for direction; news if something systemic is driving it.

**Output:** GUTCHECK REPORT, with Next steps carrying most of the weight. "This is solved and here's the fix" is a complete, valuable answer.

**Effort:** 10-15 minutes, one phase.

**Goes wrong when:** it validates the feeling and leaves them stuck. Sympathy is not a deliverable.

---

## 7. Decision analysis — "X or Y"

**When:** two or three named options and a person stuck between them.

**Questions:**
1. What criteria actually decide this for them (not the criteria they named)?
2. How does each option do on each criterion, on comparable evidence?
3. What do people who chose each one say afterwards, especially the regretters?
4. What does the choice cost to reverse?

**Evidence:** one `interest_over_time` call containing every option so they share a scale; web search for "X vs Y" and migration stories; each option's community for regrets; job ads for career or tech choices.

**Output:** **The axis that decides it**, a **comparison** (criteria as rows, options as columns, evidence in the cells), **who each option suits**, then the verdict and a recommendation. Name a winner or say plainly it's a wash and the real cost is delay.

**Effort:** 15-20 minutes, one or two phases.

**Goes wrong when:** false balance. If the evidence favours one, say so.

## 8. Deep study — dissertation grade

**When:** an academic or formal context, or someone who needs the method itself to hold up.

**Questions:** stated formally as 3-5 research questions, each with the evidence that would answer it and the limits of that evidence.

**Evidence:** everything available, plus deliberate triangulation. Each finding should be supported by two independent sources where possible, and where it isn't, that gets said.

**Output:** **Research questions**, **Method** (what was searched, where, over what window, with what terms), **Findings** per question, **Synthesis** (the GUTCHECK REPORT core), **Limitations** (sample bias, source tilt, what couldn't be reached), **What further research would settle it**, **References** with links and access dates.

**Effort:** 45+ minutes, multiple phases, plan shown and confirmed.

**Goes wrong when:** it dresses thin web evidence in academic clothing. Public search data cannot support claims of causation, and saying so is part of the method. Real academic work needs literature this toolkit cannot reach; name that and point them at Google Scholar and their library.

## 9. Trend watch

**When:** "is this a fad", or they want to keep an eye on something over time.

**Questions:**
1. What is the shape over five years: rising, plateaued, seasonal, one spike?
2. What is rising underneath the headline term?
3. Is money following attention (funding, job ads, incumbents reacting)?
4. What would tell us early that this is turning?

**Evidence:** Trends over five years, full resolution; rising related queries and topics; news for the angle; Wikipedia to test depth against hype; builder activity for whether anyone is committing.

**Output:** **Shape**, **What's rising underneath**, **Fad or trend** with the reasoning, **Leading indicators to watch** (specific, checkable), then the verdict.

**Effort:** 10-15 minutes, one phase.

**Goes wrong when:** it describes the curve without calling it. They can read a chart; they came for a judgment.

---

## Mixing and stopping

Archetypes are starting points, not cages. A case competition about a two-sided market borrows from the landscape scan. Say which one you're running and what you borrowed.

Whatever the type, stop as soon as the decision is answered. A study that runs to completion after the answer arrived is theatre.
