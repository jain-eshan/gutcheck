# Playbooks

One per kind of question. Each says what you're really answering, where to look first, what a good answer sounds like, and how this kind of question usually goes wrong.

## IDEA — "I want to build X"

**Really asking:** is there demand, who already serves it, and is there room for me.

**Order:** Reddit first (are people complaining about existing options?), then App Store or web search for who already serves this, then Trends for whether interest is growing or flat, then news and builder activity for whether money and effort are moving in.

**A good answer names:**
- What these people do today, in specifics (the app they quit, the spreadsheet they keep).
- Which narrower group has it worst.
- What every existing option is missing, not just who they are.
- Whether the market is growing, settled, or dying, and what that means for entering it.

**Usual failure:** confirming that a category is big. "Meal planning is popular" is not a finding, because the question was whether there's room. Crowded plus flat is a no for most people; crowded plus hated is a yes. Say which one it is.

**A "no" is a great outcome.** Saying so early, with the reason, saves months. Don't soften a WEAK read into MODERATE to be nice.

## PROBLEM — "X keeps happening to me"

**Really asking:** is it just me, how bad is it for everyone else, and what actually works.

**Order:** Reddit first and hardest, phrased the way a frustrated person types it. Then web search for advice threads and fixes. Trends only to see whether the problem is growing. News if it might be systemic (a rule change, an industry shift).

**A good answer names:**
- Whether this is common or unusual, honestly.
- The words people use for it, which are often different from the words they asked with.
- What people have tried, what works, and what people say doesn't.
- Whether the fix is knowledge (it's solved, they just don't know it) or a real gap (nothing works).

**Usual failure:** sympathy without practicality. They can feel validated and still be stuck. The Next steps section matters most for this kind, and "the fix is known, here it is" is a complete and useful answer.

## TOPIC — "what's going on with X"

**Really asking:** is this growing or fading, what's happening underneath, and should I pay attention.

**Order:** Trends over 5 years first (`timeframe="today 5-y"`, `response_format="full"`) so you can see shape, not noise. Then rising related queries and topics for what's moving underneath. Then news for the current angle, and Wikipedia to test hype against depth.

**A good answer names:**
- The shape: rising, plateaued, declining, seasonal, or one spike.
- What's rising underneath, which is usually more interesting than the headline term.
- Who's driving it, and whether money is following attention.
- Fad or trend, with your reasoning stated.

**Usual failure:** describing the curve without calling it. They can read a chart. Say whether it's real, and what you'd watch to know sooner.

## DECISION — "X or Y"

**Really asking:** which one should I pick, given what I care about.

**Order:** one `interest_over_time` call with **both options in it**, so they share a scale. Then web search for "X vs Y" comparisons and migration stories. Then each option's community for what people regret. Job postings when it's a career or tech choice.

**A good answer names:**
- The axis that actually decides it (jobs, momentum, ecosystem, cost, how fast they can ship), which usually isn't the one they asked about.
- Where each option wins, plainly.
- Who each choice is right for, so they can locate themselves.
- A recommendation. "It depends" without saying what it depends on is a non-answer.

**Usual failure:** false balance. If the evidence favors one clearly, say so. And if both are fine and the real cost is dithering, say that instead: sometimes the honest verdict is "either works, pick today".

## Multi-sided ideas

An idea naming two or more distinct sides (buyers and sellers, drivers and riders, clinics and patients) gets researched per side, capped at three. Each side gets its own verdict; a marketplace usually has one hard side and one easy side, and naming which is which is the whole value of the exercise.
