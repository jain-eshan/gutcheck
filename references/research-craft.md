# Research craft

How to turn signals into a view. Read this when you're forming the read, not while gathering.

## Desire is not demand

People say they want lots of things. Demand is what they already do at cost to themselves.

Ranked weakest to strongest, the evidence you'll actually see:

1. **Stated interest** — survey says 70% would use this. Nearly worthless alone.
2. **Search volume** — someone typed it. Curiosity, sometimes intent.
3. **Complaints about existing options** — they used something, it failed them. Now you're in the game.
4. **Workarounds** — spreadsheets, manual processes, duct-taped tools. A workaround is someone paying in time. Strongest free signal there is.
5. **Money already moving** — paid apps with thousands of ratings, agencies charging for it, budget lines. Demand, proven.

Say which rung the evidence sits on. "Lots of search interest" with nothing above rung 2 is a WEAK or MODERATE read, however big the numbers look.

## Name the status quo

Nothing gets built into a vacuum. Before you call an opening real, name what these people do today:

- A competing product (they'll have to switch, which costs them).
- A manual workaround (spreadsheet, group chat, a notebook).
- A person (an assistant, an accountant, a friend who knows).
- Nothing, because they've decided to live with it.

"Nothing" is the hardest competitor in the world. A problem people have settled into is a problem they won't pay to fix. Say so when you see it.

## Intensity beats breadth

A mild annoyance for a million people is a worse business than a painful one for ten thousand. Look for intensity markers in what people write: swearing, all-caps, "I've tried everything", posts written at 2am, people offering to pay in the thread, long detailed posts about a small thing.

Breadth without intensity (high search volume, calm tone everywhere) usually means a commodity, where the winner is whoever has distribution, not the best product. Worth saying out loud.

## Cross the sources, don't stack them

The read comes from patterns across sources. Common ones:

| Pattern | Usually means |
|---|---|
| Trends flat + App Store crowded with big, actively updated apps | A settled market. Real money, no opening without a wedge. |
| Trends flat + Reddit loud | Intense problem, small or hard-to-reach group. Niche, possibly lucrative. |
| Trends rising + nothing built yet | Early. Either a real wave or a fad. Check whether the rise is one spike or sustained. |
| News hot + community silent | Press ahead of reality. Often a funding cycle, not user demand. |
| Builders active (GitHub, Show HN) + users silent | Solution looking for a problem. Very common in developer tools. |
| Search rising + Wikipedia flat | Hype without depth. People are hearing the term, not studying the thing. |
| Old abandoned repos and dead apps | People tried this. Find out why they stopped before you assume you're first. |
| One app dominating with bad reviews | The best kind of opening. Demand proven, incumbent hated. |

## Find the sharper question

The question you're handed is usually broader than the evidence supports. The most useful thing you can hand back is a narrower version that's actually true.

"Is there demand for a meal planning app?" becomes "There's demand among people who already tried two and quit, and it's about repetition, not recipes." That reframe is worth more than the verdict.

## Triangulate when everything is thin

Small or ambiguous data is normal, especially for niche and B2B questions where nobody searches and nobody posts. Don't default to INSUFFICIENT_DATA just because volume is low. Instead:

- Look for **adjacent proxies**: the category above it, the tool it would replace, the job title that would buy it.
- Check whether **money exists nearby**: agencies, consultants, courses on the topic mean budget exists even when search is quiet.
- Count **how consistent** the thin evidence is. Five posts all saying the same thing beats fifty saying different things.

Say plainly when you're reasoning from proxies, and name them.

## Look for a precedent that already ran the experiment

When you can't observe your market directly, find a case where something structurally identical already happened, and check how it turned out. This is the strongest move available when direct evidence is thin, and it beats any amount of proxy data.

Where precedents hide:
- **A regulator already mandated it.** If a rule forced this exact behaviour somewhere (a neighbouring category, another country), the aftermath tells you what actually blocks it. Search for the rule, then search for what happened 12-24 months later.
- **An adjacent category did it first.** Same buyer, same workflow, different product.
- **Another country did it earlier.** Especially useful for payments, health, and anything regulated.
- **Someone tried and died.** Dead startups, abandoned repos, and discontinued features are experiments that already ran. Find the post-mortem.

Say what makes the precedent comparable and what makes it different, then be explicit that you're reasoning by analogy. A precedent that failed for a reason your case shares is close to decisive.

## Know whose data you're holding

- Surveys published by companies that sell the solution are marketing. Cite them, discount them, say who ran it.
- Reddit skews young, Western, English-speaking, and negative. People post to complain, not to say things are fine.
- App Store ratings skew to the delighted and the furious.
- Hacker News skews technical, early-adopter, and contrarian.
- Google Trends only knows what people type into Google. Whole categories (enterprise procurement, anything WeChat or WhatsApp-native) are close to invisible.

None of this makes a source useless. It makes it a source with a known tilt, and naming the tilt is what separates research from quoting.

## B2B and niche questions

Search and Reddit are thin here by nature. Better signals:

- Job postings mentioning the pain (people hire around problems they can't tool away).
- Vendor count and pricing pages in the space. Many vendors with "contact us" pricing means real budget.
- Conference talks, trade publications, industry associations.
- Regulation. A new rule creates budget faster than any trend.

Low search volume in B2B is not evidence of no demand, and saying so is one of the most valuable corrections you can make.

## Timing

Ask whether the moment is now or three years out. Signals that a wave is real rather than a spike: the rise sustains over 12+ months, adjacent categories rise too, money is moving (funding, acquisitions, job posts), and at least one incumbent is responding. A spike on one news event, with everything else flat, is a fad.

## When to say you don't know

Reach INSUFFICIENT_DATA only after you've tried proxies. But when you get there, say it early and clearly, name the one thing that would settle it, and don't pad the report with sources that didn't answer the question. An honest "this needs ten customer conversations, not more data" is a real deliverable.

## What a good verdict does

It changes what someone does on Monday. Before you finish, check your read against that bar: if they'd do exactly the same thing having read it, either the evidence is genuinely neutral (say so) or you stopped at summarizing.
